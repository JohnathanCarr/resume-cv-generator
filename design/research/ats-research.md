# Evidence, not keywords, wins the screen

Real applicant tracking systems almost never "auto-reject" a resume for how it looks. The common causes of rejection without human review are **employer-configured knockout questions and hard rules** (work authorization, license, degree, location), plus, in a minority of setups, AI match-score thresholds. Everything else that hurts candidates happens indirectly. A bad parse leaves an empty or garbled profile that recruiter searches miss. Recruiters type literal skill and title terms into search. Since 2024, AI graders at the top vendors (Workday HiredScore, Greenhouse Talent Matching, Ashby, iCIMS, Oracle) rank applicants by whether each **required and preferred qualification is clearly evidenced**. Market concentration makes this concrete: **Workday handles over 40% of Fortune 500 career sites** and Workday plus SAP SuccessFactors cover 52.4%, while Greenhouse, Workday, Lever and iCIMS each receive 14–19% of the applications Jobscan users send ([Jobscan](https://www.jobscan.co/blog/fortune-500-use-applicant-tracking-systems/)). The famous "ATS rejects 75% of resumes" figure traces back to a 2012 sales pitch with no published method. For Resume Studio, the implication is to optimize three measurable properties. The first is a clean, linear text layer that parses identically everywhere. The second is literal coverage of the posting's core terms and title wherever the profile truthfully supports them. The third is explicit, per-requirement evidence that an AI grader or a 7-second human scan can find. Keyword density, hidden text and invented qualifications have no evidence behind them and carry real downside.

A note on evidence quality runs through everything below. Almost every "ATS study" comes from a company that sells resume tools (Jobscan, Enhancv, ResumeGo, Kickresume, Resumap), and peer-reviewed work is essentially absent. The report labels claims as **documented** (vendor product documentation or court and regulator records), **measured** (a test with a disclosed method), **survey** (usually vendor-commissioned), **marketing** (a vendor's self-claim), or **folklore** (repeated advice with no test or documentation behind it).

## Workday now fronts over 40% of the Fortune 500

The only recurring measurement with a disclosed method is Jobscan's manual audit of Fortune 500 career pages. In 2026 it found a detectable ATS at **487 of 500 companies (97.4%)**, with **Workday at "over 40%"** and **SuccessFactors at 12%**. Taleo is described as declining and iCIMS as "enduring", with no figures given for either ([Jobscan 2026](https://www.jobscan.co/blog/fortune-500-use-applicant-tracking-systems/)). Detection has stayed between 97% and 99% every year since 2018. Ongig's 2019 baseline had Workday at 22.6% and Taleo at 22.4% ([Ongig](https://blog.ongig.com/?p=16664)), so Workday has nearly doubled its share in seven years, largely by absorbing Taleo and IBM BrassRing customers. Many "ATS market share" charts still circulating online recycle the 2019 Ongig data under newer dates.

Below the Fortune 500 the picture flattens. Jobscan's broader dataset covers 13,130 companies drawn from more than 1M user scans and skews toward white-collar and tech roles. In it, **Greenhouse holds 19.0%, Workday 17.5%, Lever 15.6% and iCIMS 14.4%** ([Jobscan](https://www.jobscan.co/blog/fortune-500-use-applicant-tracking-systems/)). By revenue the market is fragmented. Apps Run The World puts it at **$2.51B in 2024**, with iCIMS first at a 10.7% share and the top 10 vendors holding only 51.1% ([Apps Run The World](https://www.appsruntheworld.com/top-10-hcm-software-vendors-in-applicant-tracking-market-segment/)). In the SMB and startup tier, vendor claims stand in for measurement. Ashby is growing fast among AI and tech companies and names OpenAI, Notion and Shopify as customers ([American Bazaar](https://americanbazaaronline.com/2025/07/23/ai-hiring-platform-ashby-secures-50-million-to-streamline-recruitment-465454/)). Employ (Lever, Jobvite, JazzHR) claims 23,000+ customers ([Employ](https://www.employinc.com/?p=2002)), and BambooHR claims 30,000+ ([BambooHR](https://www.bamboohr.com/careers/bamboohr-way/)). Hourly and frontline hiring is moving to payroll-bundled systems (UKG, Dayforce, Paycom, ADP) and to chat-based front ends such as Paradox, where the resume matters less.

Consolidation in 2024–2025 pushed AI ranking into the core of the biggest suites. Workday bought **HiredScore** (AI grading) in February 2024 and **Paradox** (conversational apply) in October 2025 ([Workday](https://newsroom.workday.com/2025-10-01-Workday-Completes-Acquisition-of-Paradox)). SAP closed its **SmartRecruiters** acquisition in September 2025 ([SAP News](https://news.sap.com/2025/09/sap-completes-smartrecruiters-acquisition/)). iCIMS launched AI agents and bought Apli ([iCIMS](https://icims.com/company/newsroom/summerrelease2025)). Oracle keeps Taleo in maintenance mode while migrating customers to Oracle Recruiting Cloud ([Redress Compliance](https://redresscompliance.com/oracle-taleo-licensing-guide.html)). For a US white-collar applicant, then, a resume is most likely parsed by Workday, Greenhouse, Lever, iCIMS, SuccessFactors/SmartRecruiters or Oracle. Small-company adoption figures (the often-cited 35% or 44%) have no traceable original source ([Geekflare](https://geekflare.com/guides/ats-stats/)). No source measures what share of applications, as opposed to employers, flows through each system.

## The text layer's reading order, not the column count, decides a parse

The parser behind each ATS is mostly undisclosed. The documented cases are **Textkernel** (which absorbed Sovren and now belongs to Bullhorn), with a plug-in for Oracle Recruiting Cloud ([Textkernel](https://www.textkernel.com/integrations/oracle-recruiting-cloud/)) and a listed integration with SAP SuccessFactors ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.co.uk/software/126982/textkernel)). Workday documents its own machine-learning parsing and Skills Cloud without naming a vendor ([Workday docs](https://doc.workday.com/admin-guide/en-us/human-capital-management/recruiting/recruiting-setup/qqz1592342635105.html)). Greenhouse refers only to generic "text parsing technology" ([Greenhouse](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/200721684-Why-didn-t-the-candidate-s-resume-import-correctly)). Online claims that "iCIMS uses parser X" are unverified. A candidate cannot know which engine will read the file, so the safe target is the lowest common denominator: one clean, linear text stream.

Commercial parsers extract the same core record: contact details, work history (title, employer, dates, location, description), education, certifications, skills and languages. They output JSON or XML and derive values such as **total years of experience** from dates ([Textkernel data model](https://developer.textkernel.com/Parser/master/data_model)). Skills and titles are then normalized against taxonomies. Textkernel's has **13,000+ skills and 250,000+ synonyms** and can map to O\*NET and ISCO ([Textkernel FAQ](https://developer.textkernel.com/tx-platform/v9/faq/); [Professions API](https://developer.textkernel.com/tx-platform/v10/skills-intelligence/professions-api/overview/)). Workday does not auto-fill skills straight from the parse. Its ML suggests skills mined from the whole resume, and recruiters accept or delete them ([Workday docs](https://doc.workday.com/admin-guide/en-us/human-capital-management/recruiting/recruiting-setup/qqz1592342635105.html)). So a skill mentioned inside a bullet still counts, and canonical names match the taxonomy best.

On file types the documented facts are simple. Lever lists Word, PDF, RTF, HTML and OpenDocument as parseable and warns that if you cannot highlight the text, the file probably cannot be parsed ([Lever](https://help.lever.co/hc/en-us/articles/20087345054749)). Greenhouse fails on non-standard types, unrecognized characters, too little text and files **over 2.5 MB** ([Greenhouse](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/200989175-Unsuccessful-resume-parse)). Scanned PDFs need OCR. Textkernel sells OCR as an extra and says Oracle Recruiting Cloud lacks it natively ([Textkernel ORC blog](https://textkernel.com/learn-support/blog/oracle-recruiting-cloud-resume-parsing)). The debate over whether DOCX beats PDF in Workday is folklore with no vendor documentation on either side. What matters is a **selectable text layer**, not the file extension.

The most useful measured evidence comes from Resumap, a template vendor that disclosed its interest. It ran one synthetic resume through **36 PDF layouts and 4 real parsers** (Textkernel, Workable, Zoho Recruit, Manatal) and checked the text layer first. Email and phone parsed in 36/36 cases on every parser, and Textkernel recovered 10/10 skills in every layout. **Work-history segmentation was the most layout-sensitive field**, correct in 31/36 layouts on Textkernel. Its conclusion was that "the column count didn't predict the outcome; the emission order did" ([Resumap](https://dev.to/resumap/we-ran-the-same-resume-through-4-real-ats-parsers-in-36-layouts-same-text-different-parses-3mfc)). Smaller commercial tests on two-column layouts contradict each other ([ATS Verification](https://atsverification.com/blog/two-column-resume-ats-friendly/); [Enhancv](https://enhancv.com/blog/are-two-column-resumes-ats-friendly/)). Two more failure modes come from documentation rather than tests. PDF ligatures can extract as junk codepoints even when the page renders correctly ([Sensible](https://docs.sensible.so/docs/ligatures)). Unrecognized characters break Greenhouse's parser. Rules about specific date formats, heading names and bullet glyphs are plausible practitioner consensus with no vendor specification behind them.

A failed parse does not reject anyone. When an import fails in Greenhouse, the recruiter enters the data by hand and still sees a PDF preview of the original ([Greenhouse](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/200721684-Why-didn-t-the-candidate-s-resume-import-correctly)). Textkernel describes the cost of parse errors as **incomplete profiles with worse searchability** ([Textkernel](https://textkernel.com/learn-support/blog/oracle-recruiting-cloud-resume-parsing)). Greenhouse's AI matcher sends unscorable resumes to "Needs manual review" ([Greenhouse](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/41396009937307-Talent-Matching)). The penalty is invisibility in search and ranking, not a rejection email.

For Chrome specifically, Save as PDF has produced text-based, **tagged PDFs by default since Chrome 85**, with structure taken from the page's HTML semantics ([Chromium blog](https://blog.chromium.org/2020/07/using-chrome-to-generate-more.html)). Many parsers read the raw content stream rather than the tags, though, so semantic HTML does not prove a safe reading order. That has to be checked on real output.

## Knockout answers reject candidates; formatting does not

The "75% of resumes are rejected by ATS" statistic is **folklore**. It traces to Preptel, a resume-optimization firm that pitched it around 2012, shut down in 2013 and never published a method ([Fortune 2014](https://dc.fortune.com/2014/08/22/how-to-keep-your-resume-from-getting-lost-in-cyberspace); [Enhancv ATS Hub](https://enhancv.com/ats-hub/)). The best counter-evidence is small. Enhancv's structured interviews with **25 US recruiters** (2025) found 23 (92%) whose ATS does not auto-reject on formatting, content or design. The other 2 use match or experience thresholds, reportedly in Bullhorn and BambooHR. 44% have an AI fit score, but only 8% use it as a definitive filter ([Enhancv](https://enhancv.com/blog/does-ats-reject-resumes/)). The sample is tiny and the publisher sells an ATS checker, so treat it as a credible directional finding, not a measurement.

What does auto-disqualify candidates is documented. **Knockout questions** act on structured application answers, not on resume text. Workday's admin guide gives "automatically decline ineligible candidates" as a questionnaire-rule example ([Workday](https://doc.workday.com/admin-guide/en-us/manage-workday/business-processes/questionnaires/questionnaire-video-examples/collect-information-from-job-applicants/review-the-responses-to-a-questionnaire.html)). Ashby's auto-reject fires only at submission and stays separate from its AI review ([Ashby](https://docs.ashbyhq.com/auto-reject-applications)). SAP teaches "rejection screening questions" in its SmartRecruiters course ([SAP Learning](https://learning.sap.com/courses/smartrecruiters-for-sap-successfactors-academy/creating-rejection-screening-questions)). Workable and JOIN restrict knockouts to yes/no, select, numeric or date fields, and free text and file uploads cannot trigger rejection ([Workable](https://help.workable.com/hc/en-us/articles/115012238688); [JOIN](https://join.com/en/features/screening)). A rejection within minutes almost always means an answer about authorization, sponsorship, a license, location or a degree matched a disqualifying value.

The Harvard Business School and Accenture "Hidden Workers" study fills in the systemic picture: **88% of employers say their systems screen out qualified high-skill candidates** who don't exactly match the job-description criteria, rising to 94% for middle-skill roles ([HBS report](https://www.hbs.edu/managing-the-future-of-work/Documents/research/hiddenworkers09032021.pdf); [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/news/automated-hiring-software-is-rejecting-millions-of-suitable-job-candidates)). The filters it criticizes are criteria employers configure, such as degree requirements and gap rules, not software judging layout. The Enhancv and HBS findings therefore fit together. Software rarely rejects on its own, but rigid literal criteria, applied by rules and by recruiters, routinely do. Volume finishes the job. Recruiters estimate 400–600 applicants for entry-level roles and 2,000+ for remote tech roles, and 52% review in arrival order ([Enhancv](https://enhancv.com/blog/does-ats-reject-resumes/)), so a low-ranked resume is functionally unread.

Recruiter search is where literal keywords bite. Jobscan's survey of 384 recruiters reports that 99.7% use ATS filters, most often on **skills (76.4%), education (59.7%), job title (55.3%) and certifications (50.6%)** ([Jobscan](https://www.jobscan.co/blog/resume-keywords/)). This is a vendor survey with an unpublished method, but it matches how vendors document their products. Greenhouse's full-text resume search returns snippets of the matched terms and documents no stemming or synonym handling ([Greenhouse](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/115004600186-Search-resumes-for-keywords)). Lever reportedly stems words ("collaborating" matches "collaboration") but does not equate "SEO" with "Search Engine Optimization" (third-party claims; [Jobscan](https://www.jobscan.co/blog/lever-ats/)). Workday Skills Cloud and Textkernel do resolve synonyms against an ontology ([Workday](https://doc.workday.com/admin-guide/en-us/human-capital-management/skills-and-skills-cloud/skills/reference--types-of-skills.html); [Textkernel](https://developer.textkernel.com/SearchMatch/master/Concepts/Introduction/)). Because the tool cannot know which behavior applies, **exact posting wording plus both forms of each acronym** is the dominant strategy. Jobscan's claim that including the target job title brings **10.6× more interviews** has no published method and conflicts with its own earlier 10.2× figure. Treat it as directional only. No evidence shows that keyword repetition or density helps any system, and white-text stuffing appears in the plain-text layer that parsers and recruiters both see ([Hiration](https://www.hiration.com/blog/white-text-resume-hack/)).

## AI graders score each requirement, then humans decide

The 2024–2026 AI layer is better documented than most folklore assumes, and it all works the same way. **Workday HiredScore** grades candidates A–D by matching the resume against the job's qualifications. A means all basic (required) qualifications and most preferred ones. B means all basic. C means most but not all basic, and D means fewer than most ([Workday HiredScore](https://doc.workday.com/hiredscore/en-us/workday-hiredscore/recruiter-productivity-/reference--candidate-grades.html)). Since August 2026 each qualification is assessed individually ([release notes](https://doc.workday.com/hiredscore/en-us/workday-hiredscore/hiredscore-release-notes/2026/august/wednesday--5th-august.html)). **Greenhouse Talent Matching** sorts candidates into Strong, Good, Partial or Limited against a calibration the hiring team weights. It shows recruiters the matched skills and criteria and "does not automatically disposition any candidate" ([Greenhouse](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/41396009937307-Talent-Matching)). **Ashby** marks each applicant Meets or Does not meet for each recruiter-defined criterion and cites the supporting resume text ([Ashby](https://docs.ashbyhq.com/ai-assisted-application-review)). iCIMS Role Fit combines experience match, skills match and tiering ([iCIMS](https://www.icims.com/blog/top-5-ai-recruiting-software-capabilities-to-drive-business-value/)). Oracle's candidate score compares requirements with education, experience and skills ([Oracle](https://www.oracle.com/applications/ai-apps/hr/)).

These systems resemble semantic, per-criterion evidence checks far more than keyword counting. A resume that literally contains "Python" ten times but never shows the required "3+ years building data pipelines" earns a lower grade than one bullet that states it plainly. No independent accuracy or bias benchmark exists for any vendor's ranking, and it is unknown how many Workday customers have turned HiredScore on.

Regulation shows where the line between ranking and rejecting is being contested. **Mobley v. Workday** (N.D. Cal.) was allowed to proceed in July 2024 on the theory that Workday acts as the employer's agent. An ADEA collective of applicants aged 40+ was conditionally certified in May 2025, and in July 2025 the court held that HiredScore falls within the case's scope ([Fisher Phillips](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/news-insights/discrimination-lawsuit-over-workdays-ai-hiring-tools-can-proceed-as-class-action-6-things); [Clearinghouse](https://clearinghouse.net/case/44074/)). There is no merits ruling yet. NYC Local Law 144 is close to unenforced: an audit of 391 employers found **18 posted bias audits** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.01399v1)), and the state comptroller called enforcement ineffective ([NY OSC](https://www.osc.ny.gov/state-agencies/audits/2025/12/02/enforcement-local-law-144-automated-employment-decision-tools)). The EU AI Act classifies recruitment AI as high-risk, but obligations for stand-alone Annex III systems slipped to **December 2, 2027** ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)). Expect vendors to keep AI "assistive" and leave rejection to explicit rules. In practice, a D grade still means no human reads the resume.

## Which resume practices have evidence behind them

The table below sorts common advice by evidence strength. Strong documentation exists only for parse-safety basics and for per-requirement grading. Most formatting rules are consensus with no tests behind them, and quantitative claims about outcomes come from vendors.

| Practice | Evidence tier | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Selectable text layer, no image-only content, small file | **Documented** | Lever, Greenhouse help centers |
| Linear reading order; single column safest | **Measured** (vendor test) | Resumap 36-layout test; Ladders: multiple columns hurt human scan |
| Show each required qualification explicitly | **Documented** | HiredScore, Greenhouse, Ashby AI docs |
| Exact posting terms; acronym plus full form | **Documented** (Greenhouse literal search) + third-party | Greenhouse, Jobscan, Lever claims |
| Skills section in plain text | **Survey** + documented taxonomies | Jobscan 76.4% filter on skills; Textkernel/Workday |
| Target title near top | **Survey/marketing** | 55.3% filter by title; 10.6× unverified |
| Standard headings, Mon YYYY dates | **Folklore/consensus** | Jobscan, career centers; no vendor spec |
| Avoid ligatures, icon fonts, odd glyphs | **Documented** (general PDF) | Sensible docs; Greenhouse "unrecognized characters" |
| Tailoring per posting | **Study, weak** | Kickresume ~10%→~18% response, 51 apps per arm |
| One page | **Contradicted** by the only study | ResumeGo 2018: reviewers preferred 2 pages |
| Quantified bullets | **Folklore/consensus** | No controlled study |
| Keyword density, white text | **No support; downside** | Hidden text visible in plain-text layer |

Three of these deserve comment. On length, the only quantified study is ResumeGo's simulated review by 482 hiring professionals. It found two-page resumes preferred about 2.3× overall and about 1.4× even for entry-level roles ([ResumeGo](https://www.resumego.net/research/one-or-two-page-resumes/)). Resume Studio's one-page rule is a product choice, not an ATS requirement, so the generator should fill the page densely with relevant evidence rather than trim aggressively. On tailoring, Kickresume's small field test saw responses rise from about **10% to 18%** when an AI resume was tailored per posting ([Kickresume](https://www.kickresume.com/en/press/job-application-experiment/)). It is weak, vendor-run evidence, but it points the same way as the structural argument. On AI authorship, about **20% of hiring managers** say they would reject an AI-written resume outright ([TopResume](https://topresume.com/career-advice/ai-in-hiring-survey)), and 61% say AI resumes make candidates look more qualified than they are ([AIResumeBuilder via GoSkagit](https://www.goskagit.com/airesumebuilder-com-survey-1-in-5-employers-may-adopt-pay-to-apply-model-as-ai/article_90c6c61b-de74-58fa-a260-310f0b0d2a65.html)). No mainstream ATS documents an AI-text detector. The risk is a human reacting to generic or inflated prose, which reinforces Resume Studio's never-invent rule.

The 7-second scan from Ladders (7.4 s in 2018) has a thinly disclosed method ([ERE critique](https://www.ere.net/articles/is-the-6-second-resume-scan-a-myth)). Its qualitative finding still holds up and points the same way: recruiters fixate on **job titles, employers, dates and section headers** first ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/)).

## Requirements checklist for Resume Studio

The evidence translates into three jobs for the generator. Make the text layer boringly parseable. Make the posting's must-haves literally and explicitly present wherever the profile supports them. Refuse anything that manufactures a match. The table below gives each requirement, the check the extension can run itself, and the evidence behind it.

| # | Requirement | Measurable check the generator can run | Why (evidence) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Parse safety** | | | |
| 1 | DOM order equals visual reading order: single column, no CSS `order`, grid placement, floats or absolute positioning for content | Walk rendered blocks; assert each block's top coordinate increases in DOM order; fail on any `order`, `position:absolute`, or multi-column `grid-template-columns` in resume content | Emission order predicts segmentation (Resumap) |
| 2 | No layout tables, text boxes, SVG text or images carrying content | Assert zero `<table>`, `<img>`, `<svg>`, `<canvas>` and zero CSS `background-image` inside the resume | Image content is unparseable (Lever); RChilli advises against tables |
| 3 | Contact info as plain body text on its own line; no `@page` margin boxes, headers/footers or icon-only labels | Contact block is the first text node; email, phone, city and LinkedIn appear as literal strings matched by regex | Contact data is robust only when it is in the text stream |
| 4 | URLs written as visible text, not only as anchor labels | For every `<a href>`, its visible text contains the domain and path | Parsers read visible text, not link annotations |
| 5 | No ligatures or private-use glyphs; standard system or embedded text fonts; no icon fonts | CSS `font-variant-ligatures: none`; scan the final text for U+FB00–FB06 and U+E000–F8FF; bullets limited to `•`, `-` or list markers | Ligatures extract as junk (Sensible); Greenhouse fails on unrecognized characters |
| 6 | Standard section headings from a fixed vocabulary | Headings ∈ {Summary, Experience / Work Experience, Skills, Projects, Education, Certifications} | Consensus; field-scoped search needs correct sections (Textkernel) |
| 7 | One date format for roles ("Mar 2022 – Present"); year-only allowed for education; never invent months | Regex-validate every role date; the source date string must contain the output month if a month is shown | Consensus; derived years-of-experience depends on dates |
| 8 | Title, employer and dates on one line per role; bold titles | Structural assertion per experience entry | Ladders: titles and dates are scanned first |
| 9 | Extracted text matches intent | Round-trip test: extract the generated HTML's `innerText` and diff it against the intended content model; in CI, run `pdftotext -raw` on a sample Chrome export and verify section order, intact email/URL, and no ligature codepoints | Tags are not proof; content-stream order is what parsers read |
| 10 | Small file, no raster images | Exported PDF well under 2.5 MB (expect under 200 KB) | Greenhouse 2.5 MB parse limit |
| **Match quality** | | | |
| 11 | Extract the posting's required and preferred qualifications, core skills/tools, and title as separate lists | Store `required[]`, `preferred[]`, `skills[]` and `title` with source spans from the posting | AI graders score basic vs preferred qualifications separately (HiredScore) |
| 12 | Core-keyword coverage: each posting skill the profile supports appears verbatim at least once | `coverage = matched_supported / supported`; target 100%; report unsupported terms to the user as gaps, never insert them | Literal recruiter search (Greenhouse); 76.4% filter on skills |
| 13 | Per-requirement evidence: every supported required qualification maps to a specific bullet or skill line | Requirement → output-line mapping with zero unmapped supported requirements; show the map in the UI | HiredScore, Greenhouse and Ashby assess each criterion against cited resume text |
| 14 | Acronym and full form once each for key terms ("Search Engine Optimization (SEO)") | For each posting term with a known expansion, assert both forms are present | Lever reportedly does not bridge acronyms; taxonomies normalize either way |
| 15 | Title alignment: the posting's title appears in the headline or summary only when the profile truthfully supports it (same or adjacent role); past job titles are never changed | Headline title is either the posting title or a source title; exact string match against the profile's titles or an approved adjacency; any rename of a past title fails | 55.3% filter by title; truthfulness |
| 16 | Plain-text skills section grouped by category, posting-matched skills first, no bars or ratings graphics | Every skill in the section exists in the profile; the first N skills are posting matches | Skills are the top filter; graphics don't parse |
| 17 | Key skills also appear in context in bullets, not only in the skills list | ≥ 70% of matched core skills appear in at least one experience or project bullet | Workday mines skills from the whole resume; humans trust demonstrated skills |
| 18 | Hard requirements the user meets (license, clearance, degree, authorization, location) stated explicitly | If the posting names one and the profile has it, assert it is present in the output text | Recruiter filters and AI criteria checks look for them; knockouts mirror them |
| 19 | Front-load relevance: the most posting-relevant role and bullets come first; a 2–3 line summary built only from source facts | Ranking score per bullet; the top bullet of each role has the highest requirement overlap | F-pattern scan; summary gives a truthful home for title and top skills |
| **Never do** | | | |
| 20 | No keyword stuffing, repetition for density, or hidden or white text | Each keyword appears at most about 3 times; all text color meets contrast; no `display:none`, zero-size or off-page text | No evidence of benefit; visible in the plain-text layer |
| 21 | No invented facts, metrics, titles, dates, employers, skills or credentials | Every output claim traces to a profile field (provenance IDs); numbers in output ⊆ numbers in source | Managers penalize inflated AI resumes; never-invent product rule |
| 22 | No generic AI filler ("results-driven", "spearheaded synergies", "leveraged cutting-edge") | Lint against a banned-phrase list; flag bullets with no concrete noun, tool or number from the source | Hiring managers react to generic AI text |
| 23 | Don't sacrifice relevant substance to hit one page | Page-fill metric: content height ≥ 90% of the page; drop the least relevant lines first, never a supported required qualification | ResumeGo shows no penalty for density; one page is a product constraint |
| 24 | Don't claim "ATS score" or "pass guarantees" in the UI | Copy review | No validated ATS score exists; vendor ranking is opaque |

## Conclusion

The common mental model of a robotic gatekeeper that bins resumes over formatting is wrong in a way that matters for product design. The real gate has three parts. Structured knockout answers, which a resume cannot influence, do the hard rejecting. Literal recruiter search and per-requirement AI grading decide visibility. Volume and arrival order mean low visibility works like a rejection. So Resume Studio's main lever is not "beating the ATS" but **making truthful evidence legible**: a text stream every parser segments correctly, the posting's own words wherever the profile earns them, and a visible line of proof for each required qualification. These are the same things a hurried human and an AI grader look for, so one optimization serves both.

Two strategic points follow. First, the trend favors Resume Studio's never-invent constraint. As grading moves from keyword counts to cited, per-criterion evidence, and as Mobley-style litigation pushes vendors toward explainable matches, fabricated or stuffed content loses whatever small edge it once had and gains risk. Second, the evidence base is thin enough that the generator should rely on its own checks rather than borrowed statistics. Parse-safety can be verified deterministically on every export. Coverage and requirement mapping can be shown to the user as gaps to fill honestly. Measuring those properties directly is more defensible than any third-party "ATS score."
